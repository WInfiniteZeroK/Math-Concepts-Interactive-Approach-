1. The idea

We want the (signed) area between the graph of f and the x-axis over [a,b]. Curved regions are hard; rectangles are easy. So:

  1. Split [a,b] into n equal subintervals of width Δx=b−an, with endpoints xi=a+iΔx,i=0,1,…,n.
  2. In each subinterval [xi−1,xi] pick a sample point xi* (left end, right end, midpoint, …).
  3. Build a rectangle of height f(xi*) and add up the areas.
Sn= ∑i=1n f(xi*)Δx ⟶ ∫abf(x)dx = limn→∞ ∑i=1n f(xi*)Δx

Rectangles below the axis contribute negative area — the Riemann sum measures signed area.

① f(x)=x2 on [0,2]: compare L4 and R4. Which overestimates? Why does it depend on f being increasing?
Show explanation

With Δx=0.5: L4=0.5·(0+0.25+1+2.25)=1.75 and R4=0.5·(0.25+1+2.25+4)=3.75, while the exact area is 83≈2.667. So L4 underestimates and R4 overestimates.

Because f is increasing, the left endpoint gives the smallest value on each subinterval and the right endpoint the largest. Every left rectangle lies under the curve and every right rectangle reaches above it. For a decreasing function the roles swap. If f goes up and down, neither rule is guaranteed to over- or underestimate.

② Switch to sin⁡x on [0,2π]. Why is the sum so close to 0 even with large rectangles?
Show explanation

On [0,π] the graph is above the axis; on [π,2π] it is the mirror image below the axis. Each positive (blue) rectangle is matched by a negative (red) rectangle of the same size, so they cancel.

The sum is (up to rounding) exactly 0 for every n, and so is the exact integral: ∫02πsin⁡xdx=0. This is signed area. The total geometric area, counting both humps as positive, is 4.

③ Choose any function and note the error at n=10 and n=20 for Left vs Midpoint. What happens to each error when n doubles?

Doubling n roughly halves the Left error but quarters the Midpoint error. Left/Right errors behave like Cn; Midpoint errors behave like Cn2. The midpoint wins because, on each small piece, the part of the rectangle that sits above the curve almost cancels the part that sits below it. Section 3 shows this as the slopes −1 and −2 on the log–log plot.

3. How fast do the sums converge?

Log–log plot of |Sn−exact| against n (n=1,…,100) for the current function and interval. A straight line of slope −p means the error behaves like C/np.

Left Right Midpoint Trapezoid Dashed grey line marks your current n.

Observation: Left/Right errors shrink like 1/n (double n → error halves); Midpoint/Trapezoid shrink like 1/n2 (double n → error ÷ 4) for smooth functions. This is why numerical software never uses plain left sums.

4. Why it matters in your field

Computer science / AI
Numerical integration in code is literally a Riemann sum loop. Expected values, loss over continuous data, and rendering (light integrated over a pixel) all rely on it.
Architecture & design
Area of an irregular floor plate or façade from measured offsets: slice into strips, add up strip areas.
Engineering
Distance from sampled velocity data, work from a force sensor, total energy from a power meter reading every second.
Economics & social sciences
Consumer surplus, total revenue from a marginal revenue curve, and the Gini coefficient (area under a Lorenz curve).
