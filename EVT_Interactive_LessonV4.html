The Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)

An interactive lesson for first-year calculus: bounded vs unbounded, closed vs not closed, and applying the EVT

Extreme Value Theorem. If f is continuous on a closed, bounded interval [a,b], then f attains an absolute maximum value f(c) and an absolute minimum value f(d) for some numbers c,d∈[a,b].

1. Bounded vs Unbounded

Bounded set. A set S⊆ℝ is bounded if there is a number M>0 with |x|≤M for every x∈S. Otherwise S is unbounded.
Bounded interval. An interval is bounded if both of its endpoints are finite real numbers, e.g. [a,b], (a,b), [a,b), (a,b]. An interval involving −∞ or +∞, such as [0,∞) or ℝ=(−∞,∞), is unbounded.
Key idea. "Bounded" means some M works for every element of the set. To show a set is unbounded, show that every M fails: for each M there is an element x with |x|>M. Boundedness of the interval is one of the hypotheses of the EVT.

2. Closed vs Not Closed Intervals

Closed interval. An interval is closed if it contains every (finite) endpoint, e.g. [a,b], [a,∞). Equivalently: whenever xn∈I and xn→L, the limit L is also in I — you cannot "sneak up" on a point that is missing.
Open / neither. (a,b) excludes both endpoints (open); [a,b) and (a,b] exclude one (neither open nor closed).
Closed ≠ bounded. [0,∞) is closed but unbounded. The EVT needs both: an interval of the form [a,b] with a,b finite (a compact interval).

3. Applying the EVT

The EVT has three hypotheses. If all three hold, an absolute max and min are guaranteed:

  1. f is continuous on the interval (differentiability is not required);
  2. the interval is closed;
  3. the interval is bounded.
The EVT is an existence theorem: it says the extremes exist, not where they are. Also, the conditions are sufficient, not necessary — if a hypothesis fails, extremes may still exist.

3B. The Closed Interval Method (step by step)

To find the absolute extremes of a continuous f on [a,b]: (i) find critical points in (a,b) — where f′(x)=0 or f′(x) does not exist; (ii) evaluate f there and at the endpoints a,b; (iii) the largest value is the absolute max, the smallest is the absolute min.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to evaluate f at the endpoints.
  • Missing critical points where f′ does not exist (e.g. x23 at 0, |x| at 0).
  • Including critical points that lie outside the interval.
  • Applying the method on an open or unbounded interval and assuming extremes must exist.
  • Reporting only the x-value: the max/min value is f(c); it occurs at x=c.

4. Why it matters in your field

Almost every design or decision problem is "find the best (or worst) value of something". The EVT tells you when a best value is guaranteed to exist — before you spend effort searching for it — and the closed interval method tells you where to look: at critical points and at the boundaries, which in practice are the limits of your design space.

Computer science & Design AI
Training a model means minimising a loss. If the parameters are kept in a closed, bounded range (weight clipping, box constraints) and the loss is continuous, a best setting exists. (The EVT extends to closed, bounded sets in ℝn — you will meet this in Linear Algebra & Multivariable Calculus.)

Without bounds it can fail: for logistic regression on perfectly separable data the loss keeps decreasing as the weights grow, and no minimum is ever reached. This is one reason regularisation is used. And when you tune a hyperparameter over a range, check the ends of the range too.
Architecture design
A simply supported beam of span L under a uniform load w has bending moment M(x)=wx2(L−x) on [0,L]. It is continuous on a closed, bounded span, so a maximum moment (and hence maximum stress) exists. M′(x)=0 gives x=L2, where Mmax=wL28; at the supports (the endpoints) the moment is 0.
Engineering systems & product design
Worst-case design: peak temperature over a duty cycle [0,T], maximum stress over an operating range, or the worst output over a tolerance band [x0−δ,x0+δ]. The EVT guarantees the worst case exists. The closed interval method says check where the derivative vanishes and the ends of the range — the worst case is often at a limit, which is why engineers test "corner cases".
Economics & social sciences
Maximise profit, utility or welfare subject to constraints: production 0≤q≤Q, a budget, a price floor or ceiling. A continuous objective on a closed, bounded choice set has an optimum. Strict constraints ("fewer than", "<") or unbounded choice sets may have no optimum — see the worked example below.
The hypotheses are real-world questions. Is the model continuous (no sudden jumps such as tax brackets, shipping tiers or discrete part sizes)? Are the constraints closed (≤ rather than <)? Is the design space bounded (finite budget, size, time)? If any answer is "no", an optimum may not exist — and an optimisation solver may wander or report a misleading answer.

Worked example (economics): profit with a capacity limit

A firm's profit from producing q units is P(q)=40q−0.1q2−500, and the factory can make at most 300 units: 0≤q≤300. Find the maximum profit.

  1. Hypotheses. P is a polynomial (continuous) on the closed, bounded interval [0,300]. ✔ A maximum exists.
  2. Critical points. P′(q)=40−0.2q=0 gives q=200, which lies in (0,300).
  3. Compare.
    qtypeP(q)

    Maximum profit is 3500, at q=200 units.
Now change the constraint. Suppose capacity is only 150 units. Since P′(q)>0 on [0,150], the maximum is at the endpoint: P(150)=3250. The boundary is the answer — forgetting endpoints would miss it.
If the rule were "fewer than 150 units" (0≤q<150, a non-closed interval), profit gets arbitrarily close to 3250 but never reaches it: no maximum exists. (In practice q is a whole number, so the firm would make 149 units — but the continuous model shows why closedness matters.)
