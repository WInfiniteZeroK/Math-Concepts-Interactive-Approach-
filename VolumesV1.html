1. The idea

We want the volume of a solid. Curved solids are hard; thin slabs are easy, because a slab is almost a prism or a cylinder: its volume is about (area of its face) × (thickness). So:

  1. Split [a,b] into n equal subintervals of width Δx=b−an and cut the solid into n slabs perpendicular to the x-axis.
  2. In each subinterval pick a sample point xi*. Replace the slab by a straight prism whose face is the cross-section there, with area A(xi*).
  3. Each prism has volume A(xi*)Δx. Add them up and let the slabs get thinner.
Vn= ∑i=1n A(xi*)Δx ⟶ V= ∫abA(x)dx = limn→∞ ∑i=1n A(xi*)Δx

This is a Riemann sum where the area A(x) takes the place of the height f(x). Cavalieri's principle follows straight away: two solids whose cross-sections have equal areas at every x have equal volumes.

Three ways to slice

Cross-sections (slicing)
A flat base region sits in the plane. Slices perpendicular to the x-axis are squares, semicircles, triangles… of side s(x).
V=∫abA(x)dx
Squares: A=s2. Semicircles on diameter s: A=π8s2. Equilateral triangles: A=34s2.
Disk method
Rotate the region between a curve and the axis about that axis. Each slab is a disk of radius R, face area πR2.
V=π∫ab[R(x)]2dx
About the y-axis use horizontal slabs: V=π∫cd[R(y)]2dy.
Shell method
Rotate about an axis parallel to the strips. Each strip sweeps out a thin cylindrical shell; unrolled, it is a sheet of size 2πr×h×Δx.
V=2π∫abr(x)h(x)dx
About the x-axis use horizontal strips: V=2π∫cdr(y)h(y)dy.

The disk method is a special case of V=∫Adx, with circular cross-sections A=πR2. The shell method slices the solid differently: into nested cylinders instead of flat slabs. Its integrand 2πrh is the area of a cylinder's curved surface, not of a flat cross-section. In this lesson every region touches its axis (disks) or sits beside it (shells), so no disk has a hole in it.

Rotating about other axes

The formulas stay the same. Only the radius changes: it is always measured from the axis of rotation.

Axis of rotationMethodRadius
y=k (horizontal)Disks in x R(x)=f(x)−k if the curve is above the line, k−f(x) if below
x=c (vertical)Shells in x r(x)=x−c if the region is to the right of the axis, c−x if to the left
Common errors to watch for
  • Forgetting the π (disks) or the 2π (shells).
  • Measuring the radius from x=0 or y=0 when the axis is somewhere else.
  • Limits of integration in the wrong variable: with dy the limits are y-values.
  • Mixing variables, such as π∫x2dy without first writing x as a function of y.

② Sphere: f(x)=4−x2 on [−2,2], disks about the x-axis, Midpoint rule with n=4. Why does the sum overestimate 43πr3?
Show explanation

Squaring the radius removes the square root: A(x)=π(4−x2), a downward parabola. So V=π∫−22(4−x2)dx=32π3≈33.510, which is 43π23.

With n=4 the midpoints are ±0.5,±1.5, giving V4=π·1·(1.75+3.75+3.75+1.75)=11π≈34.558. For a function that curves downward, the midpoint value is above the average value on each piece, so the midpoint rule overestimates.

③ Shells: f(x)=x2 on [0,2]. Rotate about x=0, then x=−1, then x=3. Two of these give the same volume. Which two, and why?

The axes x=0 and x=3 give the same volume. Pappus's theorem explains it: V=2π·(distance from centroid to axis)·(area). The centroid of this region is at x‾=1.5, which is exactly 1.5 from both axes. Moving the axis to x=−1 puts it 2.5 away, so the volume grows by the factor 2.5/1.5.

④ Cross-sections: base region under x on [0,4]. Switch between the four shapes. The volumes keep the same ratios to each other — why?
Show explanation

Every shape has area A=κs2 for a constant κ, and here s2=x. So V=κ∫04xdx=8κ: squares 8, isosceles right triangles 4, equilateral triangles 23≈3.464, semicircles π≈3.142. The shape only changes the constant κ; the integral does not change.

3. Disk or shell? One solid, two integrals

Take the region bounded by y=f(x), the line y=d and the y-axis, and rotate it about the y-axis. Horizontal strips touch the axis and make disks (integrate in y). Vertical strips are parallel to the axis and make shells (integrate in x). Both must give the same volume.

Choosing a method

Axis of rotationRegion described asEasiest set-up
x-axis (horizontal)between y=f(x) and the x-axisDisks in x
x-axis (horizontal)between x=g(y) and the y-axisShells in y
y-axis (vertical)between x=g(y) and the y-axisDisks in y
y-axis (vertical)between y=f(x) and the x-axisShells in x
Rule of thumb. Strips perpendicular to the axis give disks; strips parallel to the axis give shells. Pick the direction that lets you write the strip's length with one formula and no inverse function. If one method needs the region split into several integrals (because the top or bottom boundary changes part-way), the other method often needs just one. Setting up the same volume both ways is a good check.

4. Why it matters in your field

Engineering & manufacturing
Lathe-turned parts, pipes, nozzles and pressure vessels are solids of revolution. Their mass is density × volume from a profile drawing.
Architecture & design
Domes, columns and vases are designed as profiles that are then rotated. Concrete or glass volume comes from a disk or shell integral.
Medicine
CT and MRI scans are cross-sections. Organ and tumour volumes are found by adding slice area × slice thickness: the slicing method with real data.
Computer science & 3-D printing
Slicer software cuts a model into layers, and voxel renderers add up tiny volumes. Both are ∑AΔx in code.

Worked example (design): how much does this vase hold?

A ceramic vase is 30 cm tall. Its inside is round at every height, and a designer measured the inner radius every 5 cm. Estimate how much water it holds, in litres.

height h (cm)051015202530
radius r (cm)468753.54
r2163664492512.2516
  1. Set up. Horizontal slices are disks, so V=∫030π[r(h)]2dh. Units check: cm2×cm=cm3. There is no formula for r, only seven measurements, so a sum is the only way to compute it. Here Δh=5cm.
  2. Trapezoid rule on the areas A=πr2: T6=5π[162+36+64+49+25+12.25+162]=1011.25π≈3177cm3.
  3. Simpson's rule (weights 1, 4, 2, 4, 2, 4, 1), usually more accurate for smooth shapes: S6=53π[16+144+128+196+50+49+16]=2995π3≈3136cm3.
  4. Interpret. Since 1L=1000cm3, the vase holds about 3.1 to 3.2 litres. The two rules differ by about 40 cm³, which shows how much the 5 cm spacing limits the accuracy. Measuring every 2.5 cm would narrow it.
A tempting shortcut that fails. Averaging the radii gives r‾=37.57≈5.36 cm, and πr‾2·30≈2705cm3, about 15% too small. Volume depends on r2, and the square of the average is always at most the average of the squares. You must square each radius before adding, which is exactly what π∫r2dh does.

Going further

Gabriel's horn
Rotate y=1/x, x≥1, about the x-axis. π∫1∞dxx2=π: finite volume, but infinite surface area. Try 1/x in the explorer with b=10,50,100 and watch V approach π.
Pappus's centroid theorem
Rotating a region of area A about an axis it does not cross gives V=2πd‾A, where d‾ is the distance from the centroid to the axis (task ③ in the Explore tab).
Numerical volumes
When A(x) has no antiderivative formula, or only data is known (scans, measurements), the trapezoid or Simpson's rule gives the volume. The worked example above does exactly this.
